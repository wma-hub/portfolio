Grand Exchange “GE” Terminal

Market intelligence for a 4,700-item game economy.

Scope

Context

Personal tool. I designed it; Claude wrote the code.

Delivery

Status

Context

This started as a stock market idea: could I build my own Bloomberg Terminal from free data? Without paid data feeds it would only ever be a worse Yahoo Finance, so I moved the idea to a market with better free data.

The Grand Exchange is Old School RuneScape’s player-run market: 4,700+ items, real volatility, visible volume, and nothing but a raw price API to read it with. GE Terminal reads that market every 60 seconds, grades it, tells me what to buy, hold or sell and why, and pings my phone on Discord when something needs action.

Problem

Flipping items is a decision problem with noisy data. Prices spike and crash hourly, some items dump on predictable cycles, and the wiki’s price API gives you the number and nothing else.

Every trade came down to the same question

“Is this signal real, or am I about to chase a correction?”

Solution

GE Terminal answers that with a layered signal engine. The tool pulls live and historical prices across four timeframes, scores movers on momentum and volume, and runs each one through graded buy/sell checklists before anything shows up as an action.

Grading the market before looking at the signal

A momentum scanner can be overly active and excited. Every big move looks like an opportunity, and the biggest movers are usually the worst trades: thin items where the move is two people trading at once. So the market gets graded first: range, volatility, volume, spread and sample depth each subtract from a starting score of 100. Below 45, an item is graded F, and with the Trade grade ≥ B filter on, an F never becomes an action.

The extreme case is a hard stop. If price breaks below its own 7-day floor, the zones the signal came from stop being valid. The label switches to BROKEN and the grade takes a −60 penalty, enough to fail an item on its own. It fires rarely, because once an item crashes the floor moves down to meet it; most of the filtering happens in the grading.

DECISION FORK

Surface every mover, or hold back by default?

The obvious build shows the biggest movers; that’s what a scanner is for, and it makes the tool feel alive. However, it’s also how you learn to ignore your own alerts. So the market gets graded before the signal does, and only what clears my floor reaches Discord, plus anything I already hold or watch.

Two scales, one word

Two grades run side by side and measure different things. Trade grade asks whether an item is worth trading at all, based on range, volatility, volume, spread and sample depth. It barely moves hour to hour. Signal grade asks whether now is the moment. It’s three checklist questions, rescored every refresh: Is this a dip or a trend? Is there room to move? Are buyers in control?

Verdicts that explain themselves

A verdict engine turns 7-day, 30-day and 90-day price position into plain calls, from SELL NOW to DON’T SELL, each with its reasoning attached. Nothing shows up without a letter grade and a reason. “Dump detection” classifies the drop and measures how long recovery usually takes.

How I Built It

While I don’t write Python code, I designed and directed everything the tool does: the grading rules, the checklists, the thresholds, the verdict language and the interface, and Claude helped bring this idea to life.

I can follow the Python code well enough to trace a rule from the screen back to the line that produces it, like the dump threshold above, and to catch it when the code and the design disagree. Design it, read it, push back: a loop that built all eighteen versions.

It isn’t shipped software, but it’s built like it could be: cached API calls, saved state, and custom alert thresholds.

In Use

Early versions had no exit logic at all. I’d buy into something that looked like it was climbing and then sit on it, with no sell zone and no idea whether it was coming back; ultimately, that’s what led to the verdict engine and the recovery window. The tool learned to tell me when to get out because I’d proved I couldn’t work it out myself.

Learning

I let six classification systems pile up, each with its own vocabulary. Each one made sense on its own. They were added months apart, each solving a new, real problem.

However, none of them ever got checked against the ones already there. Every card is readable, but learning one doesn’t educate you on the others. Knowing what ACCUMULATE means tells you nothing about HOLD or WAIT, even though they measure the same thing over a different window.

The fix is fewer vocabularies. Zone and Verdict merge into one percentile scale with a timeframe setting. Signal grade and trade grade stop showing as letters and only feed into tier. Six scales become three: where the price sits, whether the item is worth trading, and whether to act now.

Across eighteen versions I never stopped to ask whether they still added up to one language.
