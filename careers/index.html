Careers Experience Refresh

97 of 100 visitors never explored a single job.

Company

Workhuman

Year

2024 – 2025

Role

UI/UX Designer

Team

Web

01

Context

Workhuman’s careers experience was a thin layer over Workday. Candidates landed, clicked a job title, and left immediately for the application system. In and out in under two minutes, on average. This left Marketing with no journey data and candidates with no context.

The content to fix it already existed. It just wasn’t reachable during a job search.

02

Problem

The careers homepage drew 2,112 views in a month. Fifty-seven of those visitors went on to look at a job listing, or three in a hundred.

“Candidates treat us like a job board, not an employer brand experience.”

Analytics review — 2 min average time on site, 1.0 jobs viewed per session, 85% apply click-rate followed by immediate exit

That cost showed up in three places at once. Candidates evaluated Workhuman on job titles alone, with no culture context to self-select against. Recruiters absorbed the gap and the same questions surfaced again in first interviews. Marketing could not attribute a single application to a campaign, which meant it could not defend the recruiting budget.

“We have no idea what content drives applications.”

Marketing, stakeholder interviews

03

Solution

Candidates need different information at different moments, and the old page delivered all of it flat, at once, in no particular order. The redesign worked backward from the drop-off data: what were people looking for when they arrived, and where did the page fail to answer?

Sequencing the answer to three questions

Lead with what Workhuman actually is as a place to work, give candidates a reason to believe it, then make the right role easy to find. Three layers, each answering one question before handing off to the next:

Every careers site we looked at leads with filters. Canva, Figma, Stripe, Notion, Shopify, Atlassian. Filter-first was the safe pattern and nobody would have questioned it. But filters serve the candidate who already knows they want Product in Dublin, and that wasn’t who we were losing. 97 of every 100 visitors never opened a job, so the failure sat upstream of search. Filters stayed optional and colour-coded department tags carried the scan. The targeted searcher scrolls further than they would elsewhere. That’s a tax on the easiest user to serve, paid to keep the harder one from leaving.

Five tabs, five candidate questions

The job detail page splits into Role, Team, Culture, Benefits, and Location, each tab mapped to a question a candidate actually asks. What’s the job? Who will I work with? Will I fit in? What’s in it for me? Where and how do I work? Tabs also made content depth independent: the Benefits tab could carry video and four regional card variants without making the Role tab heavier.

Recommendations that removed the back button

A persistent right rail, Candidates Also Viewed, carries three related roles across every tab, matched on department first, then location, then seniority. It opens in a new tab so the original context survives. Jobs viewed per session went from 1.0 to three or four.

Candidates Also Viewed runs on three rules: same department, then same location, then similar seniority. A real model was the more interesting problem and the better answer, and it would have pushed launch by weeks. The crude version shipped on time, took jobs-per-session from 1.0 to three or four, and produced the click data a real model would need anyway. It recommends by category, not by candidate, so browsing sideways gets you more of what you just looked at.

05

Learning

Content is a design constraint, not a production step. The architecture assumed team profiles, benefit copy and video that didn’t exist yet, and content strategy arrived after the structure was set. The structure was right; the schedule paid for it. On anything content-shaped I now bring content in at architecture, not production.

Role Description sits first among the five tabs because I assumed it mattered most. That was never validated, and the tab analytics exist to answer exactly that. Shipping an untested order under deadline was the right call. Not going back to check it isn’t.
