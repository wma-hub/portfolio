Careers Experience Refresh

The jobs were on a different website.

Company

Workhuman

Year

2024 – 2025

Role

Designer, Information Architecture

Team

Web, with Marketing, Recruiting and HR

Screens, copy and reading order in this case study reflect the October 2025 launch. Workhuman’s careers site has changed since.

Context

After Workhuman’s rebrand, the HX (HR) team asked us to update the careers experience. The rest of the site had been rebranded while the Careers page hadn’t, and it ran on its own “flow” through Workday.

The flow: candidates landed on one page, the Careers homepage as we called it, clicked a job title and left for Workday to read it. Seven in ten sessions ended there and once they left, we couldn’t track their activity and candidates learned nothing about what it’s like to work here.

Most of the content to fix it already existed but candidates couldn’t reach it while they were looking for a job.

Cross-functional: Marketing (3), Recruiting (2), HR (2). Competitive analysis across 10 careers sites, including Canva, Figma, Stripe, Notion, Shopify and Atlassian.

Problem

In September 2025, 2,110 people reached the careers page. 582 of them went anywhere else on workhuman.com, while the other 72.4% left, most of them for Workday, where the jobs actually lived.

The jobs section barely registered because there wasn’t one. /company/careers/list/ logged zero pageviews in late August and 269 in September.

“Candidates treat us like a job board, not an employer brand experience.”

HEAP, September 2025 — 2,110 careers-homepage visitors, 582 continued on site, 72.4% exit rate.

Ultimately, that cost three groups at once: potential candidates judged us on job titles alone, while recruiters answered the same culture questions again in first interviews, and Marketing couldn’t tie a single application to a campaign, so it couldn’t justify the recruiting budget.

“We have no idea what content drives applications.”

Marketing, stakeholder interviews

Solution

Before touching the UI, I mapped what candidates were looking for when they arrived and where the old page lost them. The old experience gave candidates everything at once, flat, in no cohesive order; to resolve this, I split it into three levels, each answering one question before handing off to the next.

Sequencing the answer to three questions

“Why Workhuman?”

Careers Homepage:

The reading order is the design: testimonial video, who we are, what we value, ways of working, then jobs. The old page put every team on one page, so I broke the teams out into their own pages. Benefits and team profiles moved down to the job page, where candidates are actually asking about them.

“What roles are available?”

Open Positions page:

Filters are optional and color-coded tags do the scanning. Ex.: Product roles are pink everywhere they show up.

“Is this role for me?”

Job Detail page:

Five tabs, plus three related roles that stay put on every tab. This is where sessions stopped ending after one page.

Why filters stayed optional

Most of the ten careers sites we audited lead with filters. But filters help the candidate who already knows they want Product in Dublin, and that wasn’t who we were losing; almost nobody reached a job listing at all, so the problem existed higher in the funnel, before search.

Filters are still the first thing on Open Positions and nothing is hidden behind them. The full list loads on arrival and the tags carry the scan.

Five tabs, five candidate questions

The job page splits into Role Description, Meet the Team, Culture, Benefits and Location and each tab answers one question a candidate could ask: What’s the job? Who would I work with? Would I fit in? What do I get? Where and how would I work?

Tabs also let each section grow on its own. Benefits carries video and four regional versions without weighing down the role description and each tab has its own URL, so content ops can link, cache and edit it without a deploy.

The job page has to carry all of this because most candidates never see the homepage. From October to December, 16,503 sessions entered the careers section and only 4,436 started on the careers homepage, about 27%. Most people land on Open Positions or a single job from search or LinkedIn. For them, the job page is the employer brand.

Recommendations that removed the back button

A right rail called Candidates Also Viewed shows three related roles on every tab. It matches on department, then location, then seniority. When a department has fewer than three open roles, which at our size was most of the time, it falls back to the newest ones. This functions as a category browse and pages per session in the jobs section went from 1.0 to three or four.

Three rules instead of a model

While a real recommendation model was the more interesting problem, it would also have pushed launch by weeks. Instead, I shipped three rules on time. It tripled pages per session and started collecting the click data a real model would need. Cost: browsing sideways mostly gets you more of what you just looked at.

Impact

The jobs moved onto workhuman.com. The page that used to end seven in ten visits now sends three in four further into the site, with the same size audience.

Time on page dropped from 2m 13s to 42 seconds. People spent two minutes on the old page hunting for jobs that weren’t there, then seven in ten left. The new page does its job in about forty seconds and hands off.

Candidates now spend almost as long on a single job page, on our site, with team, benefits and location one tab away.

Traffic held steady: 2,110 people in September, about 2,013 a month from October to December. Same audience, two and a half times as many going somewhere next.

What these numbers don’t say. Open Positions didn’t exist before October, so its traffic is new by definition. Part of September’s exit rate is the same story: the only next step was Workday, and leaving for Workday counts as an exit. What I’d defend is that there’s now a next step on our own site, that Open Positions became the most-entered page in the section, and that candidates who open a job stay close to a minute and a half.

HEAP reports exit rate two ways, and both screenshots are here: 72.4% to 26.0% by user in the funnel, 69.2% to 28.4% by session in the page table. I quote the funnel, while the gap is how they count.

October had launch promotion and November had a LinkedIn push, so neither month is clean on its own. I also don’t have applications, since they live in Workday, outside HEAP, and I never got the two joined.

Learning

I planned around content last. The structure assumed team profiles, benefits copy and video that didn’t exist yet. Now I bring content in while I’m still planning the architecture.

I put Role Description first because I assumed it mattered most, and I was right: across 73 roles it outdrew the other four tabs combined, four to one. Unfortunately, I got the order after it wrong. Culture shipped third and came last, at 37 seconds of dwell against 58 for the tabs around it. With 73% of sessions skipping the homepage, candidates weren’t getting the culture story upstream either. They just didn’t want it there - therefore I would’ve shipped Meet the Team and Benefits next.

I measured what HEAP could already see, so my numbers describe my own architecture as much as candidate behavior. Marketing’s real question was which content drives applications. I should have started there, even if it meant fighting for the Workday join.

If I picked it up again, I’d revisit the whole experience. The content has changed since launch but the design hasn’t, while the rest of workhuman.com keeps moving. It’s starting to feel detached, and there’s now a year of data to design against.
